02 / Transformation under load

PageGroup: five designers to a full-service in-house agency.

An existing team with existing output obligations, rebuilt into something structurally different while it kept delivering. No pause button. This is the closer parallel to your situation, because you already have a team.

I inherited five graphic designers: one discipline, repeated five times. I rebuilt the function as specialists by discipline plus a studio manager, adding print, web, user experience, video and photography capability. No new budget arrived to pay for it. The capability was funded by efficiency taken out of the repeatable production work those five were doing, and delivered through a restructure I proposed and led. The team changed shape rather than simply growing, and what came back was disciplines the studio did not previously have.

That is the answer to always-on cadence. The studio was high volume by design, producing over a hundred videos and roughly two thousand assets a year, while holding four brands consistent and launching Thailand and Vietnam from zero.

The arithmetic makes the argument. Eleven markets multiplied by four brands across three audience tiers, at that volume, served by a team that began as five designers. Nobody covers that by working harder. Either the repeatable work is systematised, or it does not get served.

03 / Built from nothing

AVJennings: an in-house agency from nothing.

It started as an experiment. The in-house studio was set up to test whether the model could work at all, with twelve months to prove return on creative investment or be wound back. Near-total dependence on external agencies, and no in-house agency to transform. I built and led the five-person in-house agency inside a fifteen-person marketing function, serving ten project marketers who reported to the CMO, across roughly twenty communities and 250 to 300 releases a year.

The studio did not serve marketing alone. It serviced the wider business: Acquisitions, Corporate, Customer Service, and the Design team responsible for home and community design. None of those people reported to me, and none of them briefed the same way. That is the actual in-house agency problem, and it is the one that most often sinks the model.

  • Launched and rebranded estate brands repeatedly. Several project brand launches and rebrands of large established estates, each with the same demands as a masterbrand rollout: naming, identity, collateral, environmental, digital and sales channel, delivered against a release deadline.
  • Owned and managed a $1M annual budget, along with the retained agency panel and platform vendor relationships.
  • Cut external creative agency fees from $1.5M to $2M a year down to roughly $750K, with agency dependency falling from nine agencies to three genuine specialists rather than default suppliers.
  • Proved return on creative investment. Instrumented the agency against hours, work type and delivered impact, and reported to the C-suite monthly for twelve months. Each quarter the data set the improvement plan: repeatable work redesigned to take less time, some outputs rebuilt, others retired because they no longer earned their place. The released capacity went back into conceptual work.
  • Built the operating system: templates across every project and masterbrand, briefing and review workflows, approval governance, asset management and tracked hours. The studio ran at 70 to 80 percent production efficiency.
  • Owned platform transformation from a legacy CMS to a headless architecture, including the business case and executive sign-off, and stayed accountable through to post-launch rather than treating go-live as the endpoint.
  • Kept video deliberately external. That work needed producers shooting on location nationally, so a producer network was the right structure. What gets insourced is decided by the nature of the work, not by a belief that in-house is always better.

Serving internal clients across functions that have different briefing habits, different deadlines and no reporting line to you is precisely what this role is.

On keeping people. Nobody I hired or set out to keep has ever resigned: none across four years at PageGroup, one across seven at AVJennings. The philosophy is simple enough: empower people to become the best version of themselves, and build capability rather than replacing it. It also happens to be the quiet determinant of whether an AI transition works. A team that trusts its leadership will experiment. A team that does not will wait for the tools to be imposed and then work around them.

04 / The current instrument

On AI, precise rather than enthusiastic.

The method predates the instrument by more than a decade. I did not force AI into it earlier because until roughly the last twelve months the models could not survive contact with professional production standards. Making that call required a measured baseline, which most teams adopting AI now do not have.

At Tydal we built an AI-powered design system, translating brand guidelines into working design systems across several platforms so the output holds together rather than being corrected afterwards, and used it to produce a full campaign on live client work, primarily digital assets, culminating in the social executions. Production time moved from seven days to two. The honest caveat is that stakeholders and approvals do not compress at the same rate, so the end-to-end gain is smaller than the production gain.

The constraint is not the model. Generation is the part that already works. What slows a pipeline is the brief arriving unstructured, no validation of the audience before production begins, no methodology for matching the right design system to the deliverable, and no interface a non-designer can actually use. That is measurable work, and it is where I would spend the first ninety days.

Where it is genuinely useful now

  • Adaptation and resizing across formats and channels
  • Copy variation and versioning
  • Tagging, cataloguing and asset retrieval
  • First pass video assembly
  • Brief and research synthesis

Where it is not

Concept, taste and the final quality gate stay human. Not as a concession, but because they are what determines whether the output is worth producing at all. I disclose AI use to clients and deliberately do not market on it: selling the instrument dates fast and invites the obvious question of why you would not simply buy the tools yourself.

05 / First ninety days

What I would actually do.

  • Understand the gaps before proposing anything. Where the friction actually sits, from the designers and the traffic manager through to senior stakeholders and the internal clients briefing the studio. The people doing the work usually know what is broken; they are rarely asked in a structured way.
  • Measure the baseline. Where creative time goes now, by deliverable type: production hours, revision rounds, approval cycles. Without this, no AI claim afterwards can be proven.
  • Map the brand rollout as a system, not a project. What is templated, what is bespoke, what is governed centrally, what teams can self-serve.
  • Fix the front of the pipeline. Structured briefing and audience validation before work starts, with the traffic manager, because that is where the delay actually sits.
  • Draw the agency line. Explicit agreement on what the IHA owns and what the external agency does, so neither is guessing.
  • Run two AI pilots, not ten. Scoped to single repeatable tasks, measured against the baseline, with a defined decision point and a report that shows productivity, cost and quality together.

06 / Honest gaps

What I would not claim.

  • Financial services. I have not worked in it. Property is a regulated, disclosure-heavy, high-consideration category where creative clears compliance before it reaches market, and I would expect much of that to transfer. I would rather discuss where it does and does not than overstate it.
  • The AI platform is not finished. What exists is a working production system for one asset class, not an end-to-end platform. I would rather tell you that now than discover it with you in month four.

One tension in your brief worth naming. Faster, higher volume and more cost effective sits in the same paragraph as continuing to build a highly skilled creative team. Those only reconcile if the efficiency is reinvested rather than banked. Banked, a creative function gets cheaper and slowly worse. Reinvested, it buys seniority and capability, which is exactly what happened at PageGroup.
