Anyone can list what they shipped. What a product portfolio actually turns on is the calls made along the way, including the uncomfortable ones. Three of mine are below, set up so you can make them yourself before you see what I did.

Decision 01

The website was not the problem. The cost of running it was.

We shipped one to two meaningful features a year, and each one needed its own funding request. The instinct is to blame delivery. The actual constraint was that platform run cost had eaten the budget that should have been paying for improvement. Try to fix it without touching the platform.

Even at the ceiling, the legacy stack tops out at roughly two features a year. Discipline is not the missing ingredient.

I went after the cost base, not the backlog.

Eight platforms evaluated with working demo builds, vendor product teams engaged, and external reference checks including an organisation that had made the same move off Sitecore. The internal Storyblok demo took a day. The next best took weeks. That gap was the answer.

Three five-year total cost of ownership scenarios went to board level: stay, upgrade in place, or rebuild headless. Delivery ran at $390,000 through an external build partner under a RACI, steering committee and risk register, against a launch date that could not move because the licence was not being renewed.

Result: one to two features a year became five to six a month on the same envelope. Hosting fell 90 percent, from $5,000 to $500 a month. The migration was never the point. Turning a stagnant asset into a compounding one was.

Decision 02

Fixed date, fixed budget, ten sprint weeks. Something has to give.

The launch date was immovable and the budget was set. Every feature could ship at three depths. Good earns its way to Better after launch. Best is right only when a partial version would be worse than nothing. Set the depth on each and see what makes the date.

Inside the envelope.

Launches on time.

Four at Good, one at Best.

The construction status tracker shipped at Best. It came straight out of the service blueprint answering the single loudest finding in our customer research, and a half-built version would have been worse than none: telling a buyer where their home is, inaccurately, damages more trust than saying nothing.

Everything else shipped at Good and earned its way up afterwards. That was only a defensible call because the platform decision in the first section meant post-launch improvement was actually funded. Shipping at Good is a strategy when you have a road back. It is a compromise when you do not.

The site launched on its non-negotiable date, inside budget, and avoided a $60,000 licence extension. Community enquiries rose 101 percent, median page load hit 0.22 seconds, and it scored 100 out of 100 for WCAG 2.1 AA with the Centre for Inclusive Design.

Decision 03

A product I designed, two years in, and the numbers were not there.

We redesigned an augmented reality app so buyers could walk through a full-scale home from a floor plan, or place one on their own vacant lot on site. Native iOS and Android. Everyone who used it liked it. Here is what the first two years actually looked like.

I retired it in 2022.

The adoption data was telling us something specific. It was not that the experience was weak, because everyone who reached it responded well. It was that the app store sat between a buyer and the thing we wanted them to see, and no amount of marketing spend removes a step like that from a considered purchase where people browse casually before they commit.

The interaction patterns, user flows and onboarding all carried into a browser-based implementation on the website. Same capability, no download, far wider reach. The work was not wasted; the distribution model was wrong.

I would rather kill a product I designed than defend it. Knowing when something has stopped earning its place is the same discipline as knowing what to build, and it is harder to practise on your own work.

What I would want to know

The questions I would be asking in week one.

A portfolio this size is shaped as much by how it is cut and governed as by what sits in it. These are the things I cannot work out from the outside, and the answers would change how I would approach the first quarter.

Portfolio shape

How is the portfolio actually cut?

By asset class, so residential, office, retail, industrial and build to rent each have their own products? Or by journey stage across the group? That decides whether the team serves business units or owns end-to-end experiences, and they are very different jobs.

Operating model

Where does design sit relative to product?

Designers embedded in squads with a chapter for craft, or a central team that business units draw on? The first builds depth and risks inconsistency. The second holds the system and risks becoming a queue.

Prioritisation

Who arbitrates when two divisions want the same quarter?

Every prioritisation framework works until two well-funded stakeholders both have a case. What the forum is, and how much of the call sits with this role, tells me more about the job than the roadmap does.

Ownership

Who owns the experience after someone moves in?

Post-settlement and resident experience is where I have done my most useful work. Whether it sits with this portfolio or with the asset teams changes what the role can realistically improve.

Why this role

Same discipline, considerably more room.

I have never carried a product manager title. I have carried the accountability for nine years, across a portfolio, a platform investment case taken to board level, and the call to retire something I built. Titles are how organisations describe work. The decisions above are the work.

Everything here happened at a national residential developer with a tight envelope and a small team. Mirvac runs residential, office, retail, industrial and build to rent, with a digital estate to match and specialists to lead. The discipline does not change. The room does.

The post-settlement work is what I would most want to bring across. The part of the journey most developers hand to administrators is the part that decides whether a buyer becomes a referrer. In build to rent it is not a stage of the journey at all. It is the whole product.
